## Split depth instead of width

Tensor parallelism divides one operation; pipeline parallelism divides the stack of layers. Stage one runs the first layers, stage two runs the next range, and so on. A microbatch moves from one stage to the next while earlier stages begin work on later microbatches.

## The pipeline has bubbles

With p stages and m microbatches, the first and last stages cannot both be busy during warmup and cooldown. Those idle slots are pipeline bubbles. More microbatches can improve utilization, but they also increase scheduling complexity and the amount of activation state that must be held for backpropagation.

## Read the schedule, not just the layer count

The workbench shows that equal numbers of layers do not guarantee equal stage times. A stage with a wider embedding, a mixture-of-experts block, or slower communication can become the bottleneck. Good partitioning balances time and memory, not merely layer indices.

$$
T_{\text{ideal}} \approx (m + p - 1) \tau
$$

## Recap

The central idea is **Pipeline parallelism**. The implementation detail matters because it changes what the model can learn, how much memory or compute the system needs, or how confidently we can interpret its behavior. Keep the equation visible, name the controlled variables, and treat every benchmark or safeguard as part of the model’s operating definition.
